A daily planning system that survives real life

The 1-3-5 rule, light time-blocking, a ten-minute evening shutdown and a weekly review. A planning system simple enough to keep using on a bad week.

⏱ 7 min readFree

Most planning systems fail for the same reason most diets fail: they work perfectly for eleven days and then meet a real week.

A system that survives needs three properties. It has to be fast enough to run when you are tired. It has to degrade gracefully when the day goes wrong. And it has to fit on one page.

Here is one that does.

Step 1: The 1-3-5 rule

Every day gets:

  • 1 big thing — the task that makes the day a success on its own. Usually 1 to 3 hours. If you did only this, you would be satisfied.
  • 3 medium things — real work, 30 to 60 minutes each.
  • 5 small things — errands, replies, admin. Under 15 minutes each.

Nine items. Not twenty.

The value of the constraint is that it forces the prioritisation to happen at planning time rather than at 4pm when you are choosing between the urgent thing and the important one. A 20-item list is not a plan. It is a list of things you feel bad about.

Two guidelines:

One big thing means one. If you find yourself writing two, one of them is actually a medium thing wearing a costume, or you are planning two days.

Fewer is fine. Some days are 1-2-3. Meeting-heavy days might be 0-1-4. The shape adjusts; the ceiling does not.

Step 2: Give the big thing a time

You do not need to block your entire day. That produces a beautiful calendar and a frustrating afternoon, because the first interruption invalidates everything after it.

Block one thing: the big task. Pick the window where your focus is genuinely best — for most people that is the first two to three hours of the working day — and defend it.

Everything else runs off the list, not the clock.

Time Plan
08:30 Shutdown review from yesterday, glance at the list
09:00 - 11:00 Big thing (blocked, notifications off)
11:00 - 11:15 Break
11:15 - 13:00 Medium tasks 1 and 2
13:00 Lunch, away from the desk
14:00 - 15:30 Medium task 3, plus overflow from the morning
15:30 - 16:30 The five small things, batched
16:30 - 16:40 Evening shutdown

This is a template, not a schedule. The only fixed element is the morning block.

Step 3: Batch the small things

Do not scatter the five small tasks through the day. Each one costs more in context switching than in execution.

Put them in a single window, usually late afternoon when focus is lowest anyway, and run them back to back. Small tasks are the ideal work for the part of the day when deep work is no longer realistic.

The exception is David Allen's two-minute rule from Getting Things Done: if something takes under two minutes, do it now rather than writing it down. Capturing it costs more than finishing it. Note this is a different rule from the habit-building two-minute rule, which is about shrinking habits rather than clearing tasks.

Step 4: The ten-minute evening shutdown

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that makes the rest work.

At the end of the working day, spend ten minutes:

  1. Close out today. Tick what is done. Be honest about what is not.
  2. Move the leftovers. Anything unfinished gets a new date or gets deleted. Nothing stays undated. An undated task is not a commitment, it is a source of guilt.
  3. Write tomorrow's 1-3-5. Tomorrow-you should not have to decide what matters. Tomorrow-you will be tired and will pick the easiest thing on the list.
  4. Note one thing that got in the way. One line. Over a few weeks these lines show you your actual constraint, which is almost never the one you assumed.

The shutdown works because planning and doing use different mental modes. Deciding what matters while you are in the middle of doing things produces bad decisions. Deciding the night before, when the pressure is off, produces better ones.

It also closes the loop. Unfinished work you have not written down keeps running in the background. Written down and dated, it stops.

Step 5: The weekly review

Once a week, 20 to 30 minutes. Same day, same time.

  • Look back at seven days of completed tasks and habits. What actually happened, not what you intended.
  • Check your habit completion rate and heatmap. Patterns are visible weekly that are invisible daily.
  • Identify the one thing that repeatedly did not get done. Either commit to it properly or drop it. A task carried forward five times is not a task, it is a decision you are avoiding.
  • Set the coming week's single priority.
  • Clear the backlog. Delete anything you would not do if it appeared fresh today.

The weekly review is where the system corrects itself. Without it, a daily list slowly accumulates rubbish until you abandon it and start a new one, which is how most people end up with six abandoned productivity apps.

When the day falls apart

It will. Roughly one day in four goes sideways, and a system that only handles the other three is not a system.

The rule: on a broken day, do the big thing badly rather than not at all.

If the two-hour block is gone, take 20 minutes and make partial progress. If 20 minutes is gone, take five and set the thing up for tomorrow. Partial progress keeps the thread alive; zero progress means restarting cold, which costs far more than the 20 minutes did.

For habits, drop to the minimum version and take the completion. This is exactly the "never miss twice" logic described in what a streak is: the point is not to have a perfect day, it is to avoid two consecutive zero days.

And do not replan mid-collapse. Salvage what you can, then fix it properly at the shutdown.

Why this system and not a bigger one

Elaborate systems have a hidden cost: maintaining them is itself work, and it is work that feels productive while producing nothing. Every additional field, tag, and category is another reason to stop using the system during a hard week — which is precisely the week you needed it.

1-3-5, one blocked hour, ten minutes at night, twenty minutes a week. That is the whole thing. It is small enough that running it on your worst day is realistic, and that is the only test that matters.

If you are adding new habits alongside this, be realistic about the timeline — see how long it takes to form a habit — and give each habit a fixed slot rather than leaving it to find its own place in the day.
